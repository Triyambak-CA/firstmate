An unexplained ten point drift in the promoter stake

A pure offer for sale of 25.10 per cent of pre-offer capital leaves the promoter at 74.90 per cent. The stake has been 64.75 per cent since at least Mar-2024. Roughly ten percentage points, about 71 lakh shares, left the government's hands between Mar-2019 and Mar-2024, and no second offer for sale, exchange traded fund transfer or other disinvestment tranche covering that gap could be identified.

There is almost certainly a mundane explanation and this report simply could not find it. It is flagged because an investor who cannot account for how the free float was created cannot judge whether more of it is coming.

A claim this report can definitively kill

An automated search summary asserted a 9-for-1 bonus issue in Aug-2025. It did not happen. The unadjusted monthly price series runs Rs 478.05 at the end of Jul-2025 and Rs 451.25 at the end of Aug-2025. A 9:1 bonus multiplies the share count tenfold and would produce an approximately 90 per cent price discontinuity. There is none. A separate Apr-2025 corporate action that some sources labelled a bonus was an interim dividend of Rs 4.50. Do not carry that claim into any per-share model.

A documented, multi-quarter breach of board composition rules, and four separate fines

MSTC has a run of non-compliance with the listing regulation requiring a minimum number of independent directors including a woman independent director. Fines identified: about Rs 8.91 lakh from one exchange for the quarter to 30-Jun-2025; about Rs 5.43 lakh for Q3 FY26; about Rs 5.31 lakh for Q4 FY26; and about Rs 12 lakh from the other exchange for a quarter that could not be pinned down.

The cause is structural rather than negligent: only the Ministry, not MSTC's own board, can appoint directors, and every fine disclosure has the board describing the breach as beyond management's control and seeking a waiver. Three independent directors were appointed between Apr-2025 and Jun-2025, including a woman independent director, and all statutory committees were reconstituted. Fines nonetheless continued to be disclosed for Q3 and Q4 FY26, which this report could not reconcile: either they relate to the earlier breach and were merely settled later, or compliance lapsed again.

Because MSTC has an executive chairman, the rule requires independent directors to be at least half the board rather than a third, which makes the vacancy problem more consequential than it would be elsewhere. With the Director (Commercial) chair falling vacant on 01-Nov-2026, this is a live item, not history.

Remuneration

The Chairman and Managing Director's total compensation was about Rs 84 lakh for FY25, against about Rs 20 lakh for FY24, which covered only three months in the post. Against profit after tax in the Rs 218 cr to Rs 407 cr range, that is roughly 0.2 to 0.4 per cent. There is no employee stock option scheme, no equity-linked pay and no performance bonus identified, which is normal for a government company. Alignment here runs through public-sector pay scales and ministry performance agreements, not through share ownership. An investor should not expect management to behave like an owner, because it is not structured to.

Capital allocation, ten years

Three episodes define it.

  1. The legacy loss. The origin of MSTC's bad-debt history traces to a 2008-09 gold jewellery export arrangement in which six associate exporters shipped about Rs 638.20 cr of goods to 47 foreign buyers, of whom 46 defaulted. An investigating agency in Oct-2010 described the scale as over Rs 1,400 cr. MSTC filed suits aggregating Rs 658.40 cr. The profit and loss reckoning was taken just before listing, with FY19 provisioning against long-due receivables of Rs 542.08 cr, producing the Rs 307.10 cr consolidated loss in the year MSTC came to market. A separate steel and coal customer default episode in the FY18 to FY20 window could not be independently confirmed and may or may not exist alongside this one. No accountability trail was found in the public record for any MSTC director or officer. The investigation named external traders.
  2. The subsidiary disposal. Ferro Scrap Nigam Limited was sold to Konoike Transport of Japan for Rs 320 cr against a reserve price of Rs 262 cr, agreement reported around 24-Oct-2024, completion around Jan-2025. The only other qualified bidder was a domestic steel group subsidiary. Realising 22 per cent above reserve on a government strategic sale is a reasonable outcome.
  3. The distribution. FY25 dividends of Rs 45.50 per share on 7.04 cr shares is Rs 320.3 cr, against disposal proceeds of Rs 320 cr. The disposal money went out of the door. Section 17 argues about whether that was right.

MSTC has paid a dividend in each of seven consecutive years. There has been no dilution since listing, no buyback, and no acquisition. The capital allocation record is: distribute everything, reinvest almost nothing, and acquire nothing. For a business with no reinvestment outlet that is defensible. It also means there is no internal growth engine being funded, and it makes the announced entry into a travel business, discussed in Sections 12 and 15, the first genuine capital-allocation decision in years.

Section 08

Management guidance scorecard

A scorecard cannot be built, and proving why is the finding.

MSTC does hold earnings calls, but it says almost nothing forward-looking on them

Contrary to what one might assume of a small public sector undertaking, MSTC has held roughly quarterly earnings calls since about Nov-2022, hosted by a broker, giving a cadence of roughly fifteen to sixteen calls through Aug-2026. An audio recording for the Q4 FY26 call was filed, and the Q1 FY27 call was held around 14-Aug-2026. The cadence is respectable.

Two things are not. First, there was a gap of roughly three and a half years between listing in Mar-2019 and the first identified call in Nov-2022, covering precisely the years in which investors most needed to understand the legacy receivables clean-up. Second, and more materially for this section, only two forward, quantifiable commitments could be identified across the entire available record, and both are still open.

Guidance credibility ratio: not computable

With one closed item and four open or unmeasurable ones, any ratio would be a fiction. The honest statement is that MSTC does not give guidance. It does not publish a revenue target, a transacted-value target, a take-rate target, a margin target or a capital expenditure plan. Its calls are overwhelmingly a recitation of the quarter just reported.

Why the absence of guidance is a risk rather than a neutral

A company that never sets a number can never miss one, which makes it look reliable. It also means an investor has nothing to hold management to and no way to detect deterioration early.

On MSTC specifically the cost is concrete. The two numbers that actually determine the investment case, the transacted value per quarter and the realised take rate, are not guided, not tabulated consistently, and in the case of the take rate not published at all. This report had to back-solve it from two separately reported lines. That is not an acceptable disclosure standard for a business whose entire income statement is those two numbers multiplied together, and it is the single most fixable governance item on this list.

Language drift and coverage

Full question and answer text from the calls could not be reached, so the standard forensic reads on this section, tracking language from confident to hedged, identifying repeatedly dodged questions, and noting whether the chairman or a professional handles hard questions, could not be performed. They are recorded as not done rather than guessed at.

On coverage, the evidence is mixed rather than absent. Two real institutional brokers appear to carry MSTC with Neutral ratings and targets of Rs 819 and Rs 755, against a consensus of about Rs 787, though neither could be confirmed against the brokers' own published work. A larger volume of automated or content-farm targets circulates and should be disregarded entirely. Given mutual fund ownership of about 0.10 per cent and combined institutional ownership of roughly 5 to 6 per cent, coverage is certainly thin in breadth even if not literally zero.
