Section 05

Financial analysis: eight quarters and five years

Two accounting discontinuities make MSTC's reported history close to unreadable without adjustment. This section rebuilds it.

The aggregator data for MSTC is partly corrupt, and this is a live example of a known trap

The market data feed used for this report carries a quarterly series for MSTC that does not reconcile before Apr-2025. Its row labelled as the March-2024 quarter is byte-identical to its full-year FY24 row: a twelve-month figure presented as a three-month one. Its four quarters covering FY25 sum to Rs 431 cr of revenue against an annual figure of Rs 311 cr. From Apr-2025 onward the quarters sum exactly to the year and can be trusted.

Anyone computing a quarterly trend, a year-on-year growth rate or a five-year compound growth rate for MSTC from a screen without checking this will get a wrong answer. The eight-quarter table below is therefore built only from periods that reconcile, with gaps left visible rather than filled.

Revenue from operations has risen every quarter from Rs 77.43 cr to a peak of Rs 118.80 cr and then Rs 94.24 cr, which is seasonality rather than a reversal: Q4 is the strongest quarter in a government fiscal year because departments clear disposals before March. Neither headline growth number for FY26 means what it appears to mean

"Revenue up 18.9 per cent" is correct on revenue from operations, Rs 310.96 cr to Rs 369.66 cr. A widely quoted alternative figure of Rs 453.04 cr for FY26 is also correct: it is total income, which includes about Rs 83.38 cr of other income. The two are not in conflict and the apparent contradiction dissolves once you know which base each uses. This report uses Rs 369.66 cr throughout.

"Profit down 45 to 46 per cent" is arithmetically true, Rs 407.07 cr to Rs 218.43 cr, and economically meaningless. FY25 contained the subsidiary disposal gain. The company's own disclosure is that profit before tax and before exceptional items grew 22.84 per cent in FY26 to Rs 295.69 cr. That is the number a reader should anchor on, and it points the opposite way from the headline.

Revenue, margins and returns

Volume and realisation cannot be separated on the revenue line because MSTC does not publish transacted value and fee income on a matched basis every period. The nearest decomposition is the take-rate table in Section 02, and it shows FY26's revenue growth resting on a 32 per cent rise in realised rate against an 11 per cent fall in volume.

Margins are extraordinary and stable. Operating margin was 63.83 per cent in FY26 and 58.25 per cent on a trailing twelve month basis; gross margin 70.12 per cent and 58.02 per cent respectively. There is no raw material to pass through and no meaningful operating leverage left to capture, because there is almost no fixed cost to leverage. What moves the margin is mix between high-fee and low-fee auction categories.

The return question, framed properly

Asking "what is MSTC's return on capital employed" produces a misleading answer because it averages two completely different things. The platform earns roughly Rs 225 cr of operating profit on almost no capital at all: a handful of servers, 289 people, Rs 22 cr a year of capital spending. Its return on the capital genuinely at work in it is enormous, and no sensible number can be put on it.

Sitting beside that platform is roughly Rs 900 cr of shareholders' funds that are not in the platform. They are in legacy receivables, a joint venture holding, deferred tax and a treasury book, and they earn deposit-type yields while carrying Rs 1,517 cr of contingent claims. Blending the two gives a 24 per cent return on equity that describes neither.

The correct way to see MSTC is a superb, capital-free toll booth bolted to a mediocre, litigation-encumbered balance sheet. At Rs 5,103 cr the market is paying roughly 23 times operating EBIT for the toll booth and giving the balance sheet close to nothing. Section 14 tests whether that is the right trade.

Working capital, cash flow and the balance sheet

Receivables are the unresolved item. Secondary sources give Rs 407 cr at 31-Mar-2022 falling to Rs 333 cr at an unclear later date, and a much older investor-call reference to about Rs 723 cr of which only about Rs 50 cr was characterised as genuinely risky, most of the rest being covered by bank guarantees at around 110 per cent. Those figures sit on unreconciled timelines and cannot be made into a trend. No debtor-days series exists in anything reachable. What is established is that a credit rating agency's most recent available commentary still named an elongated collection period caused by long-due receivables mainly from the trading business as a live rating constraint.

A clean reconciliation, and one that raises confidence in the dividend trail

Financing outflow of Rs 53.50 cr divided by 7.04 cr shares is Rs 7.60 exactly, which is the FY26 interim dividend. That single identity establishes three things at once: the share count of 7.04 cr is right, the only dividend actually paid in cash during FY26 was the interim (the Rs 8.10 final was proposed and paid later), and MSTC did no buyback, raised no equity and repaid no debt all year.

Run the same arithmetic on FY25 and it produces something striking. Total FY25 dividends of Rs 45.50 per share on 7.04 cr shares is Rs 320.3 cr, against subsidiary disposal proceeds of Rs 320 cr. Section 17 treats what that implies about capital allocation.

And one that does not reconcile

Operating cash flow of Rs 49.54 cr against profit after tax of Rs 218.43 cr is a 23 per cent conversion. For a fee business with no inventory that would normally be a severe warning. Three benign explanations are available and none can be tested without the filed cash flow statement: interest and treasury income may be classified under investing rather than operating, which for MSTC moves a very large slice of profit out of operating cash by presentation alone; bidder deposit float moves through operating working capital, so a year of net outflow depresses it without touching economics; and tax timing.

Adding back the roughly Rs 83 cr of other income lifts the conversion to about 61 per cent, which is still short. This report does not resolve it. It is recorded as the largest single open forensic question about MSTC, and it is a question a reader can answer in ten minutes with the annual report that this environment could not open.

On the balance sheet, total assets are roughly Rs 2,118 cr to Rs 2,249 cr, net worth roughly Rs 900 cr, total debt Rs 145.23 cr. That debt figure has been identical to the rupee at Rs 145.00 cr for FY23, FY24 and FY25 and Rs 145.23 cr in FY26. A liability that does not move at all across four years is almost never live borrowing; the likely explanations are a lease liability under the accounting standard or a stale carry-forward in the data feed. It is small enough not to matter to the conclusion and is flagged rather than relied on.

The two named legacy exposures

A national audit report called an MSTC lending decision imprudent

Comptroller and Auditor General Report No. 14 of 2021 found that MSTC's decision to extend financial assistance to a steel and power company under a facilitator arrangement was imprudent, and quantified the resulting non-recovery at Rs 220.84 cr as at Feb-2021. The counterparty is reported to be in liquidation, with MSTC litigating as a claimant before the insolvency appellate tribunal, and its promoter separately named in a very large bank fraud investigation.

No evidence of any recovery or write-back in the years since could be found. This is the clearest documented instance of MSTC taking principal credit risk it was not equipped to price, which is precisely what the trading business was.

A tribunal reportedly treated MSTC's own balance sheet note as an admission of liability

Long-running litigation with two banks, spanning the Bombay High Court and the debt recovery appellate tribunal across 2019 to 2023, includes a case summary in which the debt recovery tribunal directed MSTC to pay Rs 222.51 cr, on the basis of what the tribunal treated as an admission of liability contained in MSTC's own balance sheet notes. A credit rating agency framed the same dispute at about Rs 143.62 cr in a rationale dated 30-Jan-2025.

The two figures are not additive and were not reconciled; they are most likely the same dispute at different stages or scopes. The final outcome is undetermined. Whatever the merits, the mechanism is worth stating plainly: a disclosure the company itself drafted became evidence against it. That is a disclosure-drafting governance risk independent of who ultimately wins.

What the credit ratings are telling you

MSTC carries CARE BBB+ with A2 for short term, and Acuite A+ with A1+ for short term. For a company with effectively no borrowing and a treasury book larger than its net worth, a BBB+ long-term rating is a loud statement. The constraints the agencies name are not leverage. They are the collection period on legacy receivables and the litigation. The rating agencies, who could read the filings this report could not, are not treating MSTC as a fortress balance sheet. That is the single most useful piece of third-party evidence available here, and it corroborates the direction of every flag above.

Earnings quality score: 4 out of 10

Against: contingent liabilities at roughly 1.7 times net worth; operating cash conversion of 23 per cent with no public explanation; roughly 30 per cent of pre-tax profit from other income that cannot be decomposed and which is partly earned on money belonging to bidders; a headline profit line that swung 146 per cent up and then 46 per cent down on a single disposal; a national audit finding of an imprudent lending decision with Rs 220.84 cr unrecovered; a tribunal order resting on the company's own disclosure; legacy receivables still constraining the credit rating years after the clean-up; and a credit rating of BBB+ on a debt-free balance sheet.

For: the company voluntarily discloses profit before exceptional items, which is the honest number and points the opposite way from its own headline; the dividend trail reconciles to the rupee against the financing cash flow; capital spending runs above depreciation rather than below it, so there is no sign of earnings being flattered by under-investment; and there is no evidence of aggressive expense capitalisation or of losses parked outside consolidation.

Read this score with its confidence interval

Five of the fourteen checks could not be completed because the notes to accounts were unreachable. A company whose earnings composition cannot be decomposed from public summaries scores lower on that ground alone, independently of what the true composition turns out to be. With the annual report in hand this score could reasonably move two points in either direction. It is a directional read on a pattern of flags, not a fully quantified forensic verdict, and it should not be quoted without this paragraph.
